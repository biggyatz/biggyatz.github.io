What I learned training 200+ finance professionals to use AI

A practical approach for finance teams: automate around legacy accounting systems, start with one task, keep every number traceable and keep humans signing off.

Biggyat Kumar Pandey
3 November 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: you don't need new software to use AI in finance. Automate around the system you have: start from its exports, pick one repetitive task, make every number traceable, and keep a qualified person signing off the final figures.

Between May and July 2026 I ran speaker sessions with ACCA Nepal and trained more than 200 corporate finance professionals to use AI and Python in their own work. Most of them had the same starting point: a legacy accounting system nobody was going to replace, a stack of spreadsheets around it, and a lot of repetitive work in between. This article shares the approach I teach, built from that training and from automating research workflows at Laxmi Sunrise Capital and ShareSanskar.

1. Don't rebuild the system. Automate around it.

The most common worry I heard was some version of "our software is too old for AI". It almost never is. Every accounting system can export something: a CSV, an Excel report or a PDF. That export is your starting point. A small Python script or AI-assisted workflow can pick it up, clean it, check it and produce the report people actually need. The core system stays untouched, which means no migration project, no new licences and no risk to the books.

2. Start with one task that everyone hates

The best first project is boring, repetitive and measurable. In my own work that meant:

  • A daily risk report at Laxmi Sunrise Capital that used to be assembled by hand every morning. Automating it end to end meant the desk had volatility numbers before the market opened.
  • Fundamental data locked in quarterly-report PDFs at ShareSanskar. Extracting it by hand took hundreds of hours; a set of Python scripts turned it into a scheduled job.

Time the task before you automate it. "This took four hours every Monday and now takes ten minutes" is the sentence that gets the next project approved.

3. Where AI earns its keep in a finance team

  • Extracting data from PDFs, bank statements and invoices into structured tables.
  • Matching and reconciliation: flagging the handful of entries that don't match, instead of checking every row.
  • Recurring reports that follow the same structure every week or month.
  • First drafts of commentary, such as variance explanations, for a human to edit.
  • Data checks: catching duplicates, missing values and numbers that are out of the usual range before they reach a report.

4. And where a human still has to sign off

Automation should take the drudgery, not the responsibility. In every session I am explicit about the line:

  • Final figures that go into statements, filings or decisions.
  • Judgement calls: provisions, estimates, anything that depends on context the data doesn't contain.
  • Anything regulatory or client-facing that hasn't been reviewed.

The goal is a workflow where the machine prepares and checks, and a qualified person approves. That is also what auditors want to see.

5. Every number must be traceable

Finance teams don't trust black boxes, and they shouldn't. A good automated workflow lets anyone follow a number from the final report back to the source file and the rule that changed it. That is why my sessions run on live Python prototypes: participants open the automated model and inspect it line by line, instead of taking a slide's word for it. If a reviewer can't trace it, it isn't ready.

6. Teach the method and the business case together

One line sums up how I teach: "A model is only worth what the person reading it can act on." Showing how an algorithm works isn't enough, and neither is a slide promising efficiency. People leave knowing both how it works and when to trust it, which is what lets them build the next automation themselves.

A four-week starter plan for a finance team

  1. Week 1: pick one repetitive task, write down every step, and time it.
  2. Week 2: build a small prototype on a copy of real exports. Keep it simple: a script or an AI-assisted workflow that reproduces last month's output.
  3. Week 3: run it alongside the manual process and compare the results line by line.
  4. Week 4: switch over, keep the human sign-off, and document how to trace every figure.

A note on data privacy

Client and financial data should never be pasted into a public AI chatbot. Use tools your organisation has approved, keep sensitive data on systems you control, and anonymise samples when you experiment. A workflow that leaks data saves no one any time.

Frequently asked questions

Can AI work with old accounting software?

Yes. Almost every accounting system can export CSV, Excel or PDF reports. AI and Python workflows can process those exports to clean data, run checks and produce reports, without replacing or modifying the core system.

What finance tasks should be automated first?

Start with a repetitive, rule-based task that is easy to measure, such as extracting data from PDFs, matching transactions for reconciliation, or producing a recurring weekly report.

Should AI replace human review in finance?

No. AI should prepare and check the work, while a qualified person approves final figures, judgement calls such as provisions and estimates, and anything regulatory or client-facing.

Is it safe to use ChatGPT with financial data?

Not with confidential client or company data in a public tool. Use tools your organisation has approved, keep sensitive data on systems you control, and anonymise samples when experimenting.
